# Phase 14.9.9.5 — The streets stop being perfect

> See [How it went](#how-it-went) at the end, which is where the shape it really took is written
> down. Everything above that section is the plan as it was written, including the parts the work
> proved wrong — the prediction next to the outcome is the point.

> **Amended 2026-08-20.** The tree was renumbered so that terrain could be built next, before the rest
> of M1. Below, "slot 18.5" (the per-tick recomputation cost) is now slot 19.5.

**Goal:** the benchmark can build a city whose streets are not a perfect grid, so the cost of walking
them is measured on a map that does not flatter the walk.
**Depends on:** 14.9.9.1, whose *Out of scope* is where this task comes from.
**Size:** M.

## Why now

[14.9.9.1](14.9.9.1-how-well-served-the-city-is-becomes-a-knob.md) named this as the gap it was not
closing: the pitch-4 lattice roads 45% of the city's footprint, in one component, with no dead ends,
every block the same size, and the providers spread across it so evenly that no two are ever
neighbours. Every figure the project has about the cost of the coverage was measured on it.

Coverage is a walk along the roads, and a walk is exactly the thing a perfect grid is kindest to.
There are no detours on a full lattice: the road distance between two tiles is their Manhattan
distance and nothing is ever further than it looks. Take a cross street out and every house behind it
gets further away without moving.

So the number that matters — how far a provider walks before its capacity runs out — has only ever
been measured in the best case, and slot 18.5 has to be answered on something a player would
recognise.

## What gets built

**`--seed <n>`.** Without it, the pitch-4 lattice, unchanged and still the default: every reference
figure in `bench.rs` was measured on it, and they all lose their baseline the moment the default city
changes.

**A map that is ragged but provably one piece.** The avenues run the full height and one street runs
the full width, so every avenue meets every other one whatever else happens; the remaining cross
streets are then laid a segment at a time and kept or dropped on the seed, which is free precisely
because nothing reaches anywhere only through them. Dead ends are single tiles poked into a block off
a street that is already there, so they are leaves, and a leaf disconnects nothing either. The
argument is structural — no walk over the result is needed to know it holds — and there is a test that
holds it to that over several seeds anyway.

**The buildings scattered rather than spread.** The free slots that face a road are found after the
streets are laid and shuffled, and each kind is handed a run of them. The lattice needs Bresenham to
avoid bunching; a shuffle does it already, and does it *unevenly*, which is the point.

**The map's seed is not the world's.** `World::new` keeps the seed it had, so two maps differ in their
roads and not in their births and deaths.

## Out of scope

- **Growing a street network from scratch**, with no block skeleton under it. The larger version, and
  this project's own lesson is to measure before building the expensive one.
- **Terrain.** Phase 19's, and it is what makes a map a file with a hash.
- **Rebalancing anything the ragged map turns out to break.** A finding, not a fix.

## Tests

1. A seeded map is a single road network, over several seeds — the structural argument, checked.
2. It has fewer roads than the lattice and at least one dead end, where the lattice has none of
   either.
3. The same seed builds the same city, a different seed does not, and neither is the lattice.
4. The default run, with no `--seed`, still prints the state hash it printed before.

## Verification

```sh
cargo run --release -p xtask -- bench                 # the hash must not move
cargo run --release -p xtask -- bench --seed 1
cargo test --workspace
cargo clippy --workspace --all-targets -- -D warnings && cargo fmt --all --check
cargo xtask regen-expected --check
cargo xtask doc-check
```

**Done when:** the default is untouched, a seeded map is connected and repeatable, and `A` and `G` are
recorded on the lattice and on several seeds so the difference between a perfect grid and a ragged one
is a number rather than a suspicion.

## How it went

**The suspicion was right and it is much larger than expected.** Same footprint, same 3,750 houses,
the same 1,219 providers, and **fewer** roads — and the tick costs about 40% more:

| map | road tiles | of the footprint | water covered | providers full | `A` empty tick | `G` step 3 | `G` per provider |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| lattice | 6,541 | 44% | 97% | 90% | 2.029 ms | 1.577 ms | 1,294 ns |
| seed 1 | 5,764 | 39% | 89% | 82% | 2.819 ms | 2.274 ms | 1,866 ns |
| seed 2 | 5,810 | 39% | 90% | 82% | 2.822 ms | 2.284 ms | 1,873 ns |
| seed 3 | 5,800 | 39% | 88% | 81% | 2.836 ms | 2.306 ms | 1,892 ns |

**Fewer road tiles and more work is the whole finding in one line.** A walk that visits road tiles got
12% fewer of them to visit and took 44% longer, because taking a cross street out does not remove the
houses behind it — it puts them further away. On the lattice the road distance between two tiles is
their Manhattan distance and there are no detours at all; a provider fills up early and stops. On a
ragged map it walks the detours first. Coverage falls from 97% to 89% on identical providers for the
same reason: houses go out of range without moving.

**The three seeds agree to within one percent on every column**, so this is a property of the *kind*
of map and not of a lucky draw — which is what makes it usable as a baseline rather than an anecdote.

**Every figure this project has about the cost of the coverage was taken in the best case.** That
includes the 2.12× that [14.9.9](14.9.9-a-provider-stops-looking-once-it-is-full.md) measured for the
provider stop: the stop is worth most where providers fill early, and they fill earliest on a perfect
grid. Nothing here says that number is wrong — it says it was measured on the map most favourable to
it, and slot 18.5 now has somewhere else to stand.

**Two things were argued in a comment and are now tested instead.** Connectivity, over six seeds; and
raggedness, as *fewer roads than the lattice and at least one dead end*. The second half of that test
is the half worth having: asserting the lattice has **no** dead end is what makes the seeded figure a
difference rather than a number nobody can size.

**The road share fell less than the plan implied.** "Well below 45%" turned out to be 39%, because the
avenues and one full cross street can never be dropped — they are the connectivity argument. Getting
much below that means giving the argument up and checking connectivity instead of reasoning about it,
which is the from-scratch generator this task put out of scope. The 39% still buys a 44% slowdown, so
the cheap version was worth taking first, which is what the lesson said it would be.

**`xtask` gained its first two third-party dependencies**, `rand` and `rand_pcg` — the same generator
the core seeds its own streams with rather than a second one invented here. It never touches the
simulation: it decides where the benchmark puts its roads, before the first tick.

**`preset` had already been given a `Knobs` value in 14.9.9.4**, which is why adding a fourth knob
here cost nothing. The lint that forced it was worth more than it looked.

**`ARCHITECTURE.md` is unaffected, and was looked at** — no tick step, no field on `World`, no crate;
a dependency added to `xtask` is not one added to a `sim-*` crate, and its table already says what
`xtask` is for. **`RULES.md` is unaffected**: nothing about the game changed, and how a benchmark
arranges its streets is not a rule. **`GLOSSARY.md` is unaffected**: *dead end*, *avenue* and *block*
are plain words, and no lettered measure joined `A`–`J`. `ROADMAP.md` gained this row.
