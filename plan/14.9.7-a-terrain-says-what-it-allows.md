# Phase 14.9.7 — A terrain says what it allows

> See [How it went](#how-it-went) at the end, which is where the shape it really took is written
> down. Everything above that section is the plan as it was written, including the parts the work
> proved wrong — the prediction next to the outcome is the point.

> **Amended 2026-08-20.** The tree was renumbered so that terrain could be built next, before the rest
> of M1. Below, "phase 19" (terrain: relief and cost) is now phase 16.

**Goal:** whether a building may stand on a terrain and whether a road may be laid on it are answered
by the `Terrain` enum and not by a table, with `road_cost` left where it is.
**Depends on:** nothing. It moves a rule that has been in the tables since phase 03.
**Size:** S — two methods, one struct removed, one hash regenerated.

## Why now

`terrain.ron` has carried three columns since phase 03: `buildable`, `walkable` and `road_cost`. Only
the last is balancing. Nobody will ever tune the first two, because a building standing in the middle
of a river is not a difficulty setting — it is a different game. They are part of what a terrain
**is**, and what a terrain is has been fixed in the code since the first commit: `RULES.md` already
records which terrains exist as a rule that is deliberately not tunable, and points at `grid.rs`.

Keeping them in a table is not free. Four costs, in rising order of interest:

1. `place_building` does a fallible `BTreeMap` lookup to learn a fact three match arms could state.
2. The fixture in `sim-core/tests/common/mod.rs` keeps a second copy of the whole truth table in
   Rust, and nothing keeps it in agreement with the `.ron`.
3. `CommandError::WrongTerrain` carries two meanings at once — *the ground refuses this* and *the
   tables are incomplete* — in a structured error the LLM player is meant to read and act on.
4. A `Terrain` variant added later is caught by the loader, at startup. It could be caught by the
   compiler, at the two places that decide what the game does with new ground.

The fourth is the real prize, and it is why this is worth a task rather than a tidy-up. Phase 19
spends a section arguing about whether to add terrain variants; whatever it decides, it should not be
able to add one without being made to say what may be done on it.

## What gets built

**Two methods on the enum**, `is_buildable` and `is_walkable`, both `const fn` and both an
**exhaustive `match`** rather than `matches!`. That is the load-bearing choice: `matches!` would
answer `false` for a variant added later, in silence, where an exhaustive match refuses to compile.
The naming follows `TileFlags::has_road` — no bare-adjective accessor exists anywhere in the tree.

**`TerrainDef` is deleted**, not reduced to one field. A one-field struct is room held for fields
nobody has written, and a `BTreeMap` whose `None` can only mean "the tables are broken" is a failure
mode with no caller — `tick.rs` would have to report it as a wrong terrain, which by then is false.
The validated shape becomes `[Coins; Terrain::COUNT]`, indexed by the terrain's own frozen position
so one numbering serves both the array and the hash, and `DataSet::road_cost` cannot fail. This is
already the house style: `House.satisfaction` and `Demographics.remainder` are the same shape.

**A load refusal for a cost nothing reads.** With walkability hardcoded, water's `road_cost` is
unreachable by construction: raising it to make a river expensive to cross would be ignored in
silence. The loader refuses a non-zero cost on ground no road can be laid on. The alternative was a
comment in the `.ron` explaining why a number is ignored, and a comment explaining why two things
must agree is what a check is for.

**A `doc-check` for the yes/no table in `RULES.md`.** This change quietly weakens the documents: that
table's headers are round-tripped against `terrain.ron` today by the parameter check, and afterwards
its cells are an unchecked copy of `grid.rs`. `xtask` already depends on `sim-core`, so the check
calls the two methods and compares their answers against the page — no parsing of Rust, unlike the
frozen-order check that has no such luxury.

## Out of scope

**`road_cost` stays in the table.** It is a number, and every number lives in a RON table loaded and
validated at startup (D6). If it ever starts looking tempting to hardcode too, that is the signal to
stop, because it would be the thing that turns this from a move inside D6's boundary into a break of
it.

**No new `Terrain` variant, and no change to the declaration order.** The order is frozen, the
`GLOSSARY.md` row that freezes it is untouched, and phases 19 to 21 keep the argument about new
kinds.

**Nothing pulled forward from phase 19.** No slope, no ground height, no map file.

## Tests

The hole this opens onto is rock. The existing terrain test uses water, where both answers are `no`,
so nothing in the tree distinguishes the two questions — and every recording is uniformly plain, so
nothing distinguishes a road cost read *by terrain* from one that always charges for plain ground. A
test lays a road on rock, has the building refused on the same tile, and asserts the treasury fell by
rock's price and not plain's.

Then the truth table itself, in `grid.rs`, where the answers now live; and the terrain table's
completeness rules — a terrain left out, a terrain declared twice, and the new unreachable cost —
none of which had a test, because no broken fixture ever covered terrain.

## Verification

The delicate part is the hash. `dataset_hash` feeds the **state** hash at tick 0, so dropping two
bytes per terrain moves every checkpoint of both recordings — a total diff, which is exactly what a
catastrophic regression looks like, and which blinds the protocol's *look at the first diverging
tick*.

So the work splits in two commits. The first does everything but keeps `data_hash` emitting the two
bytes, read from the enum instead of the removed fields; they are byte-for-byte the old table values,
so `regen-expected --check` has to stay **green**, and that green is the proof the refactor changed
no behaviour. The second drops them and regenerates, with the one reason stated.

**Done when:** a building is refused on rock and a road on water with neither question reaching
`DataSet`; `terrain.ron` holds `terrain` and `road_cost` and nothing else; the recordings moved once,
in the commit that says why; and `cargo test --workspace`, `cargo clippy --workspace --all-targets`,
`cargo fmt --all --check` and `cargo xtask doc-check` are all green.

## How it went

**The two-commit split did its job, and it is the part worth reusing.** The plan's worry was that a
hash change which touches every line looks identical to a regression that touches every line. Holding
`data_hash` still for one commit turned "trust me, nothing moved" into `recordings up to date,
nothing to do` — a machine-checked statement, made by the recordings, about a refactor that had
already rewritten `tick.rs`, `data.rs`, `validate.rs` and the fixture. The second commit's diff then
had the shape the plan predicted: the `.ron` files changed their `dataset_hash` line and nothing else,
the `.hashes` changed every line from the first checkpoint with the tick columns intact, and no
command list moved. Any other shape would have been the bug. The generalisation: when a change moves
a hash for a reason that is not behavioural, find a way to make the behavioural half provable on its
own first, and pay a commit for it.

**The plan predicted the wrong reason for keeping `TerrainDef`, and the right answer came from
elsewhere.** The first draft kept it as a one-field struct on the grounds that `data_hash`
destructures it exhaustively, so adding a field would stop the hash compiling — a real guard, worth
preserving. That argument is wrong, and it is wrong in an interesting way: with a single `Coins`
there is no field to forget, so the guard protects nothing. What replaced it was better on grounds the
draft had not considered at all — the `Option` that a `BTreeMap` forces on the accessor had become a
failure mode no caller could honestly report, because `is_walkable` has already answered by the time
the lookup happens. Deleting the struct removed a lie rather than relocating it.

**`WrongTerrain` recovering a single meaning was found while writing the plan, not while writing the
code.** It is the change's best consequence and it was nobody's goal: the ask was to hardcode two
booleans. It is worth recording because the error is one the LLM player is meant to act on, and an
error that means two things cannot be acted on correctly.

**The new `doc-check` exists because the change made the documents *less* safe, which was not
obvious.** `buildable` and `walkable` leaving `terrain.ron` does not break the parameter check —
its reverse direction only fires on tokens containing an underscore, so a stale `RULES.md` that still
described them as table columns would have passed. The page would have been unguarded and green. The
check that replaced that guard was tested by breaking the page on purpose — `Rock` set to buildable —
and watching it fail, because a check nobody has seen fail is not yet a check.

**The unreachable-cost refusal came in at one `if` and found nothing.** As predicted it can only
prevent a wrong belief, never a wrong simulation, since the number is unreadable by construction. It
earns its place because phase 21 is exactly when somebody will try to price a river crossing, and
without it that edit does nothing and says nothing.

**What did not need doing.** `ARCHITECTURE.md` mentions terrain nowhere, so it is consciously
declared unaffected rather than silently skipped. D6 was not amended: it binds everything *numeric*,
these are booleans, and its second sentence already puts structural rules in Rust — so this moved a
rule from the wrong side of D6 to the right side, and there was nothing in it to change.

**Review reversed the exhaustive match, and the sections above are left saying otherwise on purpose.**
*Why now* calls the compile error "the real prize" and argues it is what makes this worth a task
rather than a tidy-up; both methods are `matches!` in the tree, so a terrain added later answers no
in silence and nothing stops compiling. What is worth recording is that the argument was less load
bearing than it read. The property survives, one step later: the terrain-facts check refuses a
terrain that `RULES.md` has no row for and compares that row against the two methods, so a new kind
of ground still cannot land unnoticed — it is stopped by `doc-check` instead of by `rustc`, and the
answer has to be written on the page rather than only in code. Arguably that is the better place for
it, since the page is where somebody looks. The prize was real; it was just not where the plan
pointed.

**`Terrain::index()` arrived, against advice this plan had taken.** The design work explicitly
rejected it — the discriminant is already the frozen identity the hashes store, so a second numbering
looked like something to drift. Review asked for it anyway, and it is right, for a reason the
rejection missed: consistency is itself the safety property. Every other enum indexed by position has
`index()`, so a hand-rolled `as usize` on this one is the thing that reads as odd and gets copied.
The drift the plan feared is real but cheap to close — one test asserting `Terrain::ALL[i].index() ==
i`, the same answer `RngKind` already gives. The general lesson: "there is only one way to write
this" beats "the extra way is redundant", because redundancy you can check is not the same as
redundancy you must remember. And routing the state hash through `index()` was provable rather than
argued: `regen-expected --check` stayed green across the change, which is exactly what it means for
the two numberings to agree.

**The `RULES.md` prose went, and the table stayed.** Review removed both paragraphs around the yes/no
table and restored its backticked headers. The section is now the tile paragraph and the table alone.
The fact the deleted prose carried — that these answers are fixed in code and not tunable — is not
lost: the *rules that are not tunable* table says it, which is the page's designated home for that
kind of statement. Saying it once there beats saying it twice, and the plan's instinct to explain the
change in place was the wrong reflex on a page whose job is to state what the game does.
